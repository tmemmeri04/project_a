1 Introduction

Newtown Creek is one of the most polluted waterways in America, "1978 would mark a
pivotal year for Newtown Creek's recovery. A coast guard helicopter patrolling overhead
spotted an oil slick on the surface of the Creek that was seeping from the ground in
Greenpoint from the bulkheads at Apollo Street. What was revealed was more than 17
million gallons of oil that was covering 55 acres, the largest terrestrial oil spill in US
history, and was slowly oozing its way into the Creek." "In 2010, and in large part due
to the advocacy efforts of political and local leaders in Brooklyn, the Newtown Creek
was designated a Superfund site by the Federal United States Environmental Protection
Agency, slating it for a major cleanup to remove past industrial waste." Cleanup is delayed
until 2032. In the meantime, local groups, particularly Newtown Creek Alliance are
spearheading restoration efforts by reintroducing filter feeders and native vegetation to
the creek.

    NYS DEC's regulations state that DO must not go below 4.8 mg/L due to the health
and reproductive ability of fish and other marine orgnaisms being significantly impacted
by DO levels lower than this threshold. In this report, 5 mg/L is used as a standard. DEC
defines an equation, determined through experimentation on multiple genera of marine
organisms, which can be used to determine a time interval in days for which a body of
water may safely dip below the 4.8 mg/L threshold. This equation is more complex than
the analysis contained herein allows for, so a simple 5 mg/L threshold is considered.

    Dissolved Oxygen can vary widely based on time of day, time of year., and weather,
among other factors. This is especially the case in Newtown Creek, where the waterway
contains CSO (Combined Sewer Overflow) outlets, which dump untreated sewage into the
waterway during heavy rain events. Raw sewage contains high levels of nutrients, that
becomes food for microorganisms like algae which, after running out of those nutrients,
will die, and once they die, they will be decomposed by aerobic bacteria. It is these
bacteria which eventually consume dissolved oxygen in large amounts, and contribute in
large part to hypoxic or anoxic conditions in the waterway.

    So not only is dissolved oxygen subject to variations unique to this waterwaty, this
dataset also contained unique characteristics which were taken into account when devising
a plan to analyze the Dissolved Oxygen data, the most impactful of which is that the
NYC DEP uses 2 methods to collect data on DO in the New York Harbor.

   1. An in-situ device, Seabird CTD with DO sensor attached.

   1. Azide-modified Winkler titration method performed aboard a DEP vessel.

The in-situ sensor is highly accurate, but prone to drift, and needs calibration from time
to time. The Winkler method is viewed as the gold standard for accuracy in measuring
dissolved oxygen in water, but is also prone to error, when taking samples, care must be
taken to avoid letting oxygen dissolve in the water sample before the analysis is conducted,
the sample must ideally not be exposed to air, and also not be jostled or shaken, which
might cause oxygen to dissolve into the water. Samples are also taken at varying depths,
so a samples are divided further into "top" (1m below surface) or "bottom" (1m above
creek bottom) measurements.

    This leaves us with a dataset which is difficult to analyze as a whole. Therefore, the
decision was made to first break the data into smaller sets, along lines of measurement
method, depth, and season. Probabilities were calculated based on these groups, and
then overall probabilities were calculated afterwards for comparison.

                                                        3
    Further, this dataset refused to fit neatly into a simple model statistical distribution.
Dissolved oxygen has reason to be modeled as lognormal, as it's not possible for DO
concentrations to be negative, but there are also hard physical upper limits on the amount
of DO which can be in solution, which is generally a function of temperature (colder
water can hold more oxygen). The maximum value recorded in the dataset is a surface
measurement via the CTD machine's DO sensor, which recorded a value of 21.99 mg/L. In
general, DO in waterways is not thought of as normally distributed, in fact, it frequently
modeled using complex statistical distributions, because predicting DO levels in open
waterways means predicting one marker of a highly complex natural dynamical system,
with which humans are interacting and adding more confounding variables like CSO
events and pollution. Industrial pollution is just one of many particularly tricky variables
to deal with, because it may go unnoticed, or unreported, or people may even be trying
to hide it for a time.

    For all of these reasons, analysis using low-complexity statistical models (normal and
lognormal) were less appealing than simply using the observational data to calculate
empirically based probabilities. Multiple models were tried, none with particularly good
fit in all combinations of method, depth, season or location, but all did generally hover
around similar probabilities to that which was empirically calculated.

2.2 Probability calculations

A low-oxygen event is defined as DO at or below 5 mg/L. For each combination of
site, measurement type, depth, and season, excluding all missing readings, the empirical
probability was calculated as follows:

P (DO  5) =  number of usable readings at or below 5 mg/L               .

             number of usable readings

    Separate surface-reading and bottom-reading tables were produced, each containing
20 rows:

1. Twelve rows for three sites, two methods and two seasons.
2. Six rows combining CTD and Winkler readings within each site and season.
3. Two rows combining both methods and all three sites within each season.

    For combined groups, low-oxygen counts and reading counts are added before dividing.
The analysis does not average the individual group probabilities. Meaning, it is not
a weighted average, as there is not enough evidence available from the NYC DEP to
support a rationale for weighting the two methods. The DEP was sent a letter regarding
this matter but has not responded at the time of writing.

    Histograms were used to visualize the data as well as the logged data. But visual
analysis alone was not enough to determine wether the data were either normal, lognormal
or neither. So, Anderson Darling tests were performed on original DO and on the natural
logarithms of only positive readings (excluding zeros).

    The tests are not definitive, and are not adjusted for the possibility that multiple tests
can raise the probability of false positives, DO measurement errors or the possibility that
DO measurements are temporal dependent upon one another. They suggest less evidence
to reject normality than to reject log-normality, so are presented alongside probability
calculations which assuming normality, though obviously those probability values are not
meant to be definitive either.

2.3 Joint low-oxygen probabilities

The questions which this report is meant to answer required samples to be matched by
date and time. Luckily, a very high proportion of the measurements which occurred
on the same day as one another were taken within 60 minutes. The objective was to
analyze probabilities of simultaneous measurements, and 60 minute windows were deemed
worthy of being dubbed "simultaneous." Some days in the dataset had multiple rounds
of measurements, care was taken to separate these into matching groups as well.

                              5
    While doing the calculations to obtain the answers to the questions: "What is the
probability of not meeting standards simultaneously at all sites? Probability of exactly
one of any of the sites not meeting the standard?" Only matched sampling events with
usable DO at all three sites were retained. Calculations are performed separately for the
four measurement types, pooling all seasons and sampled years.

    For each measurement type, the denominator is the number of complete three-site
events. The two numerators count:

    · Events where all three site readings are at or below 5 mg/L.

    · Events where exactly one site reading is at or below 5 mg/L and the other two
       readings are above 5 mg/L.

    Each count is divided by the same denominator (total number of 3 simultaneous
measurement events). These, too, are direct empirical frequencies; it is assumed that si-
multaneous readings at sites along the same waterway, not far from one another, must not
be statistically independent. Therefore, empirical calculation was deemed more reliable
than fitting to a binomial distribution, which would otherwise seem the logical choice.

                      Pall three = P (DONC1  5, DONC2  5, DONC3  5) .

    A table of p-values was generated using this t-test method, p-values below 0.05 indi-
cate that there is not enough evidence to say that the two means are different from one
another at the 95% confidence level. Although it is both likely that the paired sampling
events dependent upon one another, and that there may be temporal dependence in the
data (persistent environmental conditions could affect multiple measurement events in a
row), the paired t-test only accounts for dependence among the paired values, and not
temporal dependence. The p-values were not adjusted for multiple comparisons.

3 Results and Analysis

Due to the reasons discussed in the introduction, the data was separated into smaller
groups, and probabilities were calculated separately for method, depth, location and
season. The goal of presenting the probabilities in this way is to give the whole picture.

    In the analysis, test method and sites are combined at points to illustrate overall
probabilities, but without weighting sites or methods, because no scientific justification
could be found for using weighting some sights or methods any higher than others. How-
ever, depth and season are never combined, as fluctuations across seasons are so large
that if seasons were combined, the calculated probabilities would be largely meaningless.
It is important to note that the reason the analysis is conducted using the smaller con-
stituents was to preserve potential differences that could become obscured by pooling
sites and methods from the beginning.

    The issue is however, breaking the dataset into so many smaller pieces also contributes
to a factor of meaninglessness. So in an effort to give full context, and make things more
easily understood, methods and sites are finally combined to give one seasonal probability
per season and depth. An initial visualization showing the real distribution of the data
when sample sites and methods are combined may be useful for helping situate the
calculated results into proper context.

methods and sites groups. While there is most likely some temporal dependence in the

the DEP states in the Harbor quality report, the winkler method is used to calibrate
the CTD method, which may introduce some dependence. So the CP method may be
more trustworthy for the separated groups, and less trustworthy for the combined groups
confidence interval, even though it is smaller for the combined groups.

Estimates for the likelihoods of all three sites falling below the threshold, and for exactly
one of any of the three sites falling below the threshold are presented on the table below.

    These calculation results are interesting. Firstly, the probability for all three sites
falling below the threshold at the top and bottom appears to be about one third, with
the notable exception of the CTD bottom method, which puts it at over one half. The
probabilities across both winkler groups and the CTD top also appear to be largely in
agreement, but the CTD bottom measurement shows a lower probability. This may point
to a higher likelihood of failure for one or more other site if one site fails, and perhaps
warrants a Bayesian statistical analysis. There is also the possibility of the CTD bottom
sensor having an issue, or being calibrated less often due to its depth.

    Before discussing the results of these calculations any further, the limitations of this
analysis must be acknowledged. As described in the procedure section, these calculations
are made from a dataset which matches measurements which took place on the same
day. When samples appear in the data across sites and methods, most of those samples
were taken within 60 minutes of one another. They are referred to as simultaneous, but
DO levels can fluctuate rapidly throughout the day, particularly with water temperature,
so it must be recognized that these measurements are not truly simultaneous, and some
unquantified error is contained therein.

    Secondly, there may be a reason why measurements were taken at all three locations,
as opposed to only one or another. Thst reason could be consistent across many or most

             11
of these matched sampling events. Particularly, it could be the case that samples were
collected at all three locations on days where weather was good, or waters were calm
enough to sail for an extended period of time. This could create a bias in the dataset
by leaving out the unmatched data which might have been collected under different
environmental conditions.

    This dataset also examines data across all seasons of every year, it doesn't take into
account the possibility of varying probabilities across seasons (it may be more likely that
all 3 fail in the winter for instance). There are many other ways this data is inadequate
for representing a true probability, including but not limited to the measurement errors
and uncertainties related to instruments and methods around the threshold of 5 mg/L,
and the previously discussed likelihood of temporal dependence.

    Calculating change across days, weeks or months is treated the same in pursuit of an
average DO change per day. This actually flattens real daily fluctuations. In the current
matched data, intervals are positive, the median gap is seven days, and some gaps extend
for months. Rates calculated over long gaps smooth fluctuations that may have occurred
within the interval. For instance, some gaps between samples were as long as several
months.
